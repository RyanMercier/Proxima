\begin{abstract}
Byzantine fault tolerant (BFT) protocols compare validator state with
collision-resistant hashes, which reveal whether two views differ but not
by how much. We study what a protocol gains from measuring \emph{how much},
and what it must never do with that measurement. The measurement is a
Rademacher sketch: each transaction maps to a pseudorandom vector in
$\{-1,+1\}^{n}$ and a set to the integer sum, so the squared distance
between two sketches over $n$ is an unbiased estimate of the symmetric
difference (the AMS tug-of-war estimator). We show that this sketch
cannot be binding at any modulus, because its sums never wrap, and pair
it with an LtHash accumulator that is; one ledger digest carries both.
We then give an explicit polynomial-time attack that makes an arbitrarily
large difference read as roughly a dozen transactions at $n = 512$, which
settles where estimation belongs: scheduling, synchronization, and
monitoring, never safety. On a two-phase locking BFT core with view
change we add a speculative one-round fast path at the quorum
$\lceil (n + 3f + 1)/2 \rceil$, prove and fuzz its safety (0 violations
in 50,000 adversarial executions), and show that the $2f+1$ fast path of
our earlier version is unsafe across view changes (about 4\% of fuzzed
executions decide conflicting values). Around the core we build readiness
sensing, partition detection and healing, $O(\log H)$ fork localization,
and a cross-shard conservation invariant that detects dropped, replayed,
minted, and misrouted credits exactly and localizes them by group
testing. An honest evaluation against HotStuff, HotStuff-2, chained
HotStuff, and Kauri finds no message or bandwidth advantage: the
augmentation costs about $46\%$ more bytes than HotStuff-2 at
$N = 1000$ and concentrates load on the leader. What it buys is
visibility into who is behind, by how much, and where, at a price we
report exactly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{W}{hen} two validators hash their transaction sets and
the hashes differ, a BFT protocol learns one bit. It does not learn
whether one transaction is missing or half the block, whether one
validator is behind or the network has split in two, or which of sixty
cross-shard corridors lost a credit. Modern protocols route around this
blindness well. HotStuff~\cite{yin2019hotstuff} and
HotStuff-2~\cite{malkhi2023hotstuff2} vote on block hashes after
dissemination; DAG protocols such as Narwhal~\cite{narwhal},
Bullshark~\cite{bullshark}, and Autobahn~\cite{giridharan2024autobahn}
separate data availability from ordering so that votes never wait on
mempool convergence. None of them, however, can \emph{observe}
disagreement short of reconciling it, and several decisions would
benefit from observation: when to propose so that a one-round fast path
will fire, whom to synchronize first, whether a stall is a slow
validator or a partition, and which shard pair broke settlement.

This article asks what a protocol gains from a cheap, mergeable
measurement of disagreement, and what it must never do with one. The
answers are deliberately conservative, and several of them correct our
earlier work~\cite{mercier2026proxima}.

\textbf{The measurement and its limits (Sections~\ref{sec:primitive},
\ref{sec:security}).} The measurement is the AMS tug-of-war
sketch~\cite{ams1996} with random-oracle signs: a set's sketch is the
integer sum of per-item $\pm 1$ vectors, and squared sketch distance over
$n$ is an unbiased estimate of the symmetric difference. The estimator is
classical; PBS~\cite{gong2020pbs} already uses it to size set
reconciliation. Our first observation concerns binding: because every
coordinate of a $\pm 1$ sum is bounded by the item count, reducing modulo
$q$ never wraps for any realistic set, a modular collision \emph{is} an
integer collision, and the modulus contributes nothing. The lattice
analyses that protect LtHash~\cite{lewi2019lthash} therefore do not
transfer. We keep the sketch for estimation only and pair it with a
uniform-coordinate LtHash accumulator for equality; a ledger digest
carries both. Our second observation concerns robustness: an adversary
who knows the salt and chooses which side of a difference each new item
lands on can make any number of differing items read as a constant. At
$n = 512$, greedy balancing over 256 candidates per step holds the
estimate near 14 whether the true difference is 200 or 1600
(Fig.~\ref{fig:deflation}). This is an explicit, measured instance of
known impossibility results for sketches under adversarial
inputs~\cite{mironov2011sketching,ajtai2022whitebox,hardt2013robust}, and
it fixes the architecture: estimates may gate scheduling,
synchronization, and monitoring, and must fail soft everywhere.

\textbf{A safe fast path (Section~\ref{sec:consensus}).} Estimation
earns its keep where it decides \emph{when} to try something optimistic.
Our earlier version finalized in one round once $2N/3$ validators signed
a proposal. That rule is safe within a view and unsafe across views: a
fast decision seen by one honest validator can be invisible to the next
leader. We place the fast path on a two-phase locking core with view
change, use the fast quorum $\nf = \lceil (n + 3f + 1)/2 \rceil$ of
FaB~\cite{martin2006fab} and SBFT~\cite{gueta2019sbft}, and give the
view-change rule that recovers fast decisions. Our fuzzer, which plays
equivocating leaders, double-voting replicas, and adversarial
scheduling, finds conflicting decisions in about 4\% of executions of
the $2f+1$ rule and none in 50,000 executions of the corrected protocol.
It also found a bug in our own first draft of the corrected rule
(Section~\ref{sec:consensus}). At optimal resilience $n = 3f + 1$ the
safe fast quorum is all $n$ validators; the fast path is useful when the
configured $f$ is below $n/3$.

\textbf{Observability (Sections~\ref{sec:observe}, \ref{sec:conservation}).}
On that core the sketch supports readiness sensing (propose when the
gossiped sketches say \nf\ validators already hold the block), partition
detection from bimodal sketch clouds with healing at cost proportional to
the divergence, $O(\log H)$ fork localization on prefix accumulators, and
a cross-shard conservation invariant. The last is double-entry
bookkeeping with homomorphic digests: outbound and inbound corridor
accumulators must balance once a settlement window has passed. Keying
items by corridor and indexing them by debit height makes detection exact
for dropped, replayed, minted, and misrouted credits and immune to honest
settlement lag, and group testing localizes faulty corridors.

\textbf{An honest evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).} Our earlier
version reported message counts $2\times$ below HotStuff. Under one
shared cost model, with certificates delivered to every validator and
stragglers fetching missing transactions by id, the augmented protocol
sends as many messages as HotStuff-2, about 46\% more bytes at
$N = 1000$, and loads its leader more; chained HotStuff is the cheapest
protocol we measured. Conservation verification is cheaper than
per-corridor batch roots only on dense corridor graphs (from 34 shards on
a full mesh). We report these results because they locate the
contribution correctly: observability, not efficiency.

All code, the 59 executable checks, and the script that produces every
figure and number are public, and results are bit-reproducible.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{BFT and fast paths.} PBFT~\cite{castro1999pbft} established
practical BFT; HotStuff~\cite{yin2019hotstuff} made it linear with
leader-based aggregation, HotStuff-2~\cite{malkhi2023hotstuff2} removed a
phase, and Tendermint~\cite{buchman2016tendermint} has a similar locking
structure. One-round decisions descend from FaB
Paxos~\cite{martin2006fab}, Zyzzyva~\cite{kotla2007zyzzyva}, and
SBFT~\cite{gueta2019sbft}; Kuznetsov, Tonkikh, and
Zhang~\cite{kuznetsov2021fast} settle the resilience needed for
two-step Byzantine consensus. Our fast-path quorum and view-change rule
follow this line; our contribution is the estimation layer that decides
when to attempt it, and a correction of our own earlier rule.

\textbf{Dissemination and DAG protocols.} Narwhal~\cite{narwhal},
Bullshark~\cite{bullshark}, and Autobahn~\cite{giridharan2024autobahn}
decouple data availability from consensus, so validators vote on
certified batches rather than on synchronized mempools. This removes the
throughput cost that motivated our earlier version and is the main reason
we no longer claim efficiency. Observability composes with these designs:
the same sketches can summarize which certified batches a validator
holds. Kauri~\cite{kauri} aggregates over trees with reconfiguration
on relay failure; our tree mode uses the same fallback.

\textbf{Multiset hashing.} Additive multiset hashes start with
AdHash~\cite{bellare1997adhash}, whose moduli Wagner's generalized
birthday attack~\cite{wagner2002gbp} forces to be large; Clarke et
al.~\cite{clarke2003multiset} formalize incremental multiset hashing, and
LtHash~\cite{lewi2019lthash} vectorizes it over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^{16}}^{1024}$
with an SIS-style analysis~\cite{ajtai1996sis}. LtHash is deployed for
update propagation at Meta and as Solana's accounts hash~\cite{simd0215},
so homomorphic ledger digests inside consensus are not new. Uniform
coordinates make LtHash differences uniform, hence distance-free; this is
why we carry a separate sketch.

\textbf{Sketches under adversaries.} The estimator is the AMS
sketch~\cite{ams1996} applied to set indicators. Mironov, Naor, and
Segev~\cite{mironov2011sketching} study sketching when the adversary
knows the public randomness; Ajtai et al.~\cite{ajtai2022whitebox} prove
that white-box adversaries force linear space for $F_{2}$ approximation
and show computational assumptions can restore robustness; Hardt and
Woodruff~\cite{hardt2013robust} show linear sketches fail under adaptive
queries. Robust property-preserving
hashing~\cite{boyle2019pph,fleischhacker2021pph,holmgren2022pph} gives
hashes whose distance predicates hold against adversaries, at output
sizes growing with the threshold; Holmgren et al.\ pair a decodable
syndrome with a homomorphic collision-resistant hash, the same pattern
as our estimate-then-decode synchronization. Our deflation attack is a
concrete, measured instance in the public-salt setting; we claim no new
impossibility.

\textbf{Set reconciliation.} Characteristic
polynomials~\cite{minsky2003}, IBLTs~\cite{goodrich2011iblt}, the Strata
estimator~\cite{eppstein2011strata}, Graphene~\cite{ozisik2019graphene},
Erlay~\cite{naumenko2019erlay}, and PBS~\cite{gong2020pbs} cover exact
reconciliation and difference estimation; rateless
IBLTs~\cite{yang2024rateless}, CertainSync~\cite{keniagin2025certainsync},
and self-measuring IBLTs~\cite{wu2026ibltmeasure} remove the need for a
separate estimate. Range-based reconciliation~\cite{meyer2023rbsr} bisects
monoid fingerprints, the structure of our fork localization. Dold and
Grothoff~\cite{dold2017setunion} run estimate-then-reconcile inside
Byzantine set-union consensus.

\textbf{Robust location and gossip.} Coordinate-wise medians come from
Byzantine-robust learning~\cite{blanchard2017krum,yin2018robust} and
multidimensional approximate agreement~\cite{mendes2013approx}; push-sum
gossip~\cite{kempe2003gossip} computes the averages our sketches admit.

\textbf{Sharding.} Receipt-based settlement~\cite{nightshade} carries
metadata proportional to volume; data-availability
sharding~\cite{danksharding} addresses a different layer. We found no
prior use of homomorphic accumulators as a cross-shard conservation
check.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

There are $n$ validators, at most $f$ Byzantine, with $n \ge 3f + 1$;
the adversary is static and coordinates the Byzantine validators.
Communication is partially synchronous~\cite{dwork1988partialsync}.
Validators hold BLS~\cite{boneh2004bls} keys over BLS12-381; signatures
on the same message aggregate. SHA-512 and SHAKE-256 are random oracles.
A proposal lists the ids of its transactions; an honest validator that
lacks some of them when the proposal arrives (probability
$p_{\mathrm{miss}}$, a swept parameter, missing at most $k_{\max} = 2$)
fetches them by id from the leader before voting. A block is a set and
validation rejects duplicates.

\section{The Ledger Digest}
\label{sec:primitive}

\subsection{Estimation: the Rademacher sketch}

With salt $s$, each item $x$ maps to
$v_{s}(x) = \mathrm{sgn}(H(\textsf{dom} \,\|\, s \,\|\, x)) \in
\{-1,+1\}^{n}$, the first $n$ bits of SHA-512 mapped to $\pm 1$, and a
set $T$ to $D_{s}(T) = \sum_{x \in T} v_{s}(x)$, computed over the
integers and sent as $n$ 16-bit words ($n = 512$, 1\,KB). Sketches add,
subtract, and aggregate exactly; no floating point enters any decision.

\begin{lemma}[AMS estimator for sets]
\label{lem:unbiased}
For sets $A, B$ fixed before $s$ is revealed, with $d = |A \triangle B|$
and $\dd = D_{s}(A) - D_{s}(B)$, the estimate $\hat d = \norm{\dd}^{2}/n$
satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat d] = d$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\hat d) =
2d(d-1)/n$, so its relative standard error is at most $\sqrt{2/n}$. For
multisets with multiplicity difference $c$, $\mathbb{E}[\hat d] =
\norm{c}_{2}^{2}$, not $\norm{c}_{1}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{IEEEproof}
Items in $A \cap B$ cancel; the $d$ remaining vectors enter with fixed
signs, and the coordinates of $\dd$ are independent sums of $d$
Rademacher variables $S$ with $\mathbb{E}S^{2} = d$ and $\mathbb{E}S^{4} =
3d^{2} - 2d$. Summing over $n$ coordinates gives the mean $nd$ and
variance $n(2d^{2} - 2d)$ of $\norm{\dd}^{2}$. With multiplicities the
per-coordinate sum is $\sum_{i} c_{i}\varepsilon_{i}$, whose second
moment is $\norm{c}_{2}^{2}$.
\end{IEEEproof}

The small-$d$ laws are exact and serve as test vectors: $\norm{\dd}^{2}
= n$ at $d = 1$; $4\,\mathrm{Bin}(n, 1/2)$ at $d = 2$; $n +
8\,\mathrm{Bin}(n, 1/4)$ at $d = 3$. A substitution of $j$ items reads as
$2j$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:estimator}); the nonnegative coordinates of our
earlier version let substitutions partially cancel.

\textbf{Straggler threshold.} The integer test $\norm{\dd}^{2} \le 3n$
classifies \emph{honest} divergence from a reference. Computing the
distributions exactly (Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}), an honest view with
$d = 2$ exceeds $3n$ with probability $1.6 \times 10^{-31}$ (Hoeffding
gives $e^{-n/8}$; the deviation is $n/4$ binomial units), a view with
$d = 4$ falls at or below it with probability $4.9 \times 10^{-7}$, and
$d = 3$ is a coin flip ($0.48$ above). At $n = 128$ the corresponding
values are $2.1 \times 10^{-9}$ and $8.3 \times 10^{-3}$. The threshold
says nothing about Byzantine validators, who can submit any vector,
including the reference itself.

\textbf{Salts.} Round sketches are salted with the height and the
previous block hash, which nobody knows before the previous block is
final, so no table can be built in advance. Lemma~\ref{lem:unbiased}
requires the sets to be fixed before the salt is known; where a party
chooses its set after seeing the salt, the estimate is advisory
(Section~\ref{sec:security}).

\subsection{Binding: why the sketch cannot do it}

A ledger needs an accumulator whose equality is binding: prefix digests
for fork localization, conservation checks, and prune accounting
(a node proves it pruned exactly $P$ by showing $D(\mathrm{before}) -
D(\mathrm{after}) = D(P)$). Our earlier drafts used the sketch modulo
$q = 2^{32}$ and appealed to the SIS analysis of LtHash. That appeal
fails.

\begin{proposition}[The modulus is inert]
\label{prop:inert}
Let $V$ be any matrix with entries in $\{-1, +1\}$ and $z$ an integer
vector with $\norm{z}_{1} < q/2$. Then $Vz \equiv 0 \pmod q$ if and only
if $Vz = 0$ over $\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
Each coordinate of $Vz$ has absolute value at most $\norm{z}_{1} < q/2$,
and the only multiple of $q$ in $(-q/2, q/2)$ is $0$.
\end{IEEEproof}

Every collision between realistic sets has $\norm{z}_{1} < 2^{31}$, so
binding for the sketch is the problem of finding a signed subset of
random $\pm 1$ vectors that sums to zero over the integers. Lattice
reduction is not the cheapest attack here; a generalized-birthday attack
over integer sums is, and a first-cut estimate puts it near $2^{110}$
work when long histories allow large solution weights, far below the
$n \log_{2} q$ budget the earlier text implied. We did not pursue
refinements. Instead the ledger digest binds with LtHash: each item
expands through SHAKE-256 to a uniform vector in
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^{16}}^{1024}$, exactly the parameters analyzed by Lewi et
al.~\cite{lewi2019lthash}, whose security argument applies to sets
verbatim. A \emph{ledger digest} is the pair (LtHash, sketch): both are
linear, equality is decided by LtHash alone, and the sketch reports how
large a difference is. It costs 4\,KB on the wire (2\,KB each).

\section{What the Sketch Cannot Promise}
\label{sec:security}

\begin{proposition}[Greedy deflation]
\label{prop:deflate}
An adversary who knows the salt can produce sets $X, Y$ with
$|X \triangle Y| = m$ for any $m$ such that $\norm{D(X) - D(Y)}^{2}/n$
stays below a level that depends on $n$ and the adversary's per-step
search budget $K$ but not on $m$, using $mK$ hash evaluations.
\end{proposition}

The construction keeps the running difference $S = D(X) - D(Y)$; at each
step it draws $K$ fresh items and adds the one, to whichever side, that
most reduces $\norm{S}$. A random vector's inner product with $S$ has
spread about $\norm{S}$, so each step removes roughly the squared
maximum of $K$ such products and adds $n$; the two balance at a
steady-state norm independent of $m$. Fig.~\ref{fig:deflation} measures
it at $n = 512$: with $K = 256$ the estimate stays between 11 and 14 for
true differences from 50 to 1600; even $K = 1$, where the adversary only
chooses the side, plateaus near 180. The pair-grinding analysis in our
earlier drafts, which bounded deflation to constant factors at
exponential cost, considered the wrong attack.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig3_deflation.png}
\caption{Greedy deflation at $n = 512$: reported $\hat d$ against the true
symmetric difference $m$ for per-step search budgets $K = 1, 16, 256$.
The estimate plateaus independent of $m$.}
\label{fig:deflation}
\end{figure}

Two consequences follow. First, no decision whose \emph{safety} depends
on the estimate is admissible; this is the trivial direction (sets at
distance one already differ in a transaction a protocol may care about)
made quantitative. Robust property-preserving
hashes~\cite{boyle2019pph,fleischhacker2021pph,holmgren2022pph} show that
a different object with a larger output can certify distance thresholds
against adversaries; the AMS sketch is not that object. Second, every
consumer of an estimate must fail soft. In our protocols an underestimate
undersizes an IBLT, which then doubles (extra rounds); misleads the
sensing proposer into proposing early (a lost fast path); or hides a
partition until quorum failure reveals it (the status quo). Where an
estimate must withstand chosen inputs, the parties should commit to their
sets before the salt is drawn (\emph{commit, then salt}), restoring the
hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:unbiased}.

\section{Consensus: a Base Protocol and a Safe Fast Path}
\label{sec:consensus}

\subsection{The base protocol $\Pi$}

$\Pi$ is single-slot agreement in the two-phase locking style of PBFT,
Tendermint, and HotStuff-2. In view $v$ the leader collects
\textsc{new-view} messages from $n - f$ validators, each carrying the
sender's lock (the highest prepare certificate it holds) and its
prepare-vote history; it proposes the value $\mathrm{select}$ forces, or
any value if none is forced, attaching the \textsc{new-view} set.
Validators vote \textsc{prepare} once per view if the proposal equals
$\mathrm{select}$ of the attached set and their lock permits it (the
proposal matches the lock, or the forcing evidence is from a view at
least as high). $n - f$ prepare votes form a prepare certificate, on
which validators lock and vote \textsc{commit}; $n - f$ commit votes
decide.

\subsection{The fast path}

If one view gathers $\nf = \lceil (n + 3f + 1)/2 \rceil$ prepare votes
for a value, those votes are a decision certificate: one voting round.
$\mathrm{select}$ must then recover fast decisions:

\begin{definition}[select]
Scan views from highest to lowest. At the first view holding a prepare
certificate, return its value; at the first view in which some value has
at least $\nf - 2f$ claimed prepare votes in the \textsc{new-view} set,
return that value. If neither occurs, the leader is free.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Safety]
\label{thm:safety}
If at most $f$ validators are Byzantine, no two honest validators decide
different values.
\end{theorem}

\begin{IEEEproof}[Proof sketch]
Suppose $x$ is decided first in view $w$. If by fast certificate, at
least $\nf - f$ honest validators voted $x$ in $w$; any $n - f$
\textsc{new-view} set omits at most $f$ of them, so $x$ has at least
$\nf - 2f$ claims for view $w$, while any $y \ne x$ has at most $f$
(Byzantine claims) plus $n - \nf$ (honest votes cast elsewhere), which is
below $\nf - 2f$ because $2\nf > n + 3f$. If by commit certificate, at
least $n - 2f \ge f + 1$ honest validators lock on a prepare certificate
for $x$ from view $w$, and every $n - f$ set contains one. A prepare
certificate and a fast certificate in the same view share at least
$\nf - f > f$ honest voters, so they name the same value. By induction
over later views, honest validators vote only for $x$: any valid
\textsc{new-view} set forces $x$ at view $w$, holds no certificate for
another value in a later view, and holds at most $f < \nf - 2f$ claims
for one. Hence every later certificate, fast or slow, is for $x$.
\end{IEEEproof}

Two details matter, and our fuzzer found both. The first is the quorum:
with $\nf = n - f = 2f + 1$, the rule of our earlier version, the
counting step fails. A minimal counterexample: with $n = 4$,
$f = 1$, a Byzantine leader equivocates, the Byzantine validator and two
honest validators vote $y$, one honest validator sees the three votes
and decides $y$; in the next view the leader's \textsc{new-view} set
holds the Byzantine (now claiming $x$), the honest $x$-voter, and one
$y$-voter, so $x$ is forced and the slow path decides $x$. The second is
the history: our first implementation of the corrected rule sent only
each validator's latest vote. At $n = 6$, $f = 1$ the fuzzer found an
execution in which honest validators kept voting $x$ in later
low-turnout views, each below threshold, overwriting the view-$w$
evidence until a leader was free to propose $y$. Reporting the vote
history (in a deployment, the votes cast since the validator's lock), as
FaB and SBFT do, closes it. Table~\ref{tab:fuzz} summarizes the fuzzing:
10,000 executions per configuration with equivocating leaders,
double-voting validators that lie in \textsc{new-view}, random partial
delivery for four asynchronous views, then synchronous views until an
honest leader decides.

\textbf{The price.} At $n = 3f + 1$ the safe fast quorum is $n$: a single
abstaining validator disables the fast path, as for every one-round
protocol at optimal resilience~\cite{kuznetsov2021fast}. The fast path
pays when deployments configure $f$ below $n/3$; with $n = 100$ and
$f = 20$, $\nf = 81$, so up to 19 validators may be slow or abstain.

\textbf{Where the sketch enters.} Prepare votes may carry the voter's
sketch of the proposal; the reference is $D(B)$, which anyone computes
from the block, so no party has discretion over it. The sketches report
who was behind and by how much; they never gate a vote or a certificate.
The fast-path trigger is a signature count, so a Byzantine validator that
signs correctly but submits a misleading sketch cannot suppress it.
The variance trigger of our earlier version could be suppressed by one
such validator (Fig.~\ref{fig:fastpath}, left).

\begin{proposition}[Liveness after GST]
\label{prop:liveness}
After GST, a view with an honest leader decides through the slow path:
the $n - f$ honest validators obtain missing transactions by id within
the view timeout and vote. The fast path fires iff at least \nf\ valid
prepare votes arrive before the fast timeout; with $b$ correctly signing
Byzantine validators and each of $h$ honest validators independently
holding the full block with probability $1 - p_{\mathrm{miss}}$, this is
$\Pr[\mathrm{Bin}(h, 1 - p_{\mathrm{miss}}) \ge \nf - b]$.
\end{proposition}

The liveness theorem of our earlier version bounded the failure
probability by $\exp(-2N(1/3 - p)^{2})$, which ignores the $f$ Byzantine
abstainers: at $f = \lfloor (N-1)/3 \rfloor$ the honest validators
\emph{are} the quorum, and with $p = 0.01$ at $N = 1000$ the probability
that at least one of them is missing at a fixed deadline exceeds $0.99$,
not $10^{-91}$. Correct statements count the slack $n - f - q$ explicitly,
as above, and rely on synchronization rather than on luck.

\textbf{Tree mode.} Validators form leaves of ten; the first member of
each leaf is its leader, and validators are placed at random, so some
leaf leaders are Byzantine. Votes aggregate up the tree; a leaf whose
leader is silent times out and its members send directly to the root, as
in Kauri. Leaf summaries of sketches are exact integers.

\section{Observability}
\label{sec:observe}

\textbf{Readiness sensing.} Each gossip interval, validators send the
proposer the sketch of their view of the candidate block (1\,KB each,
charged in full). The proposer proposes when at least \nf\ sketches equal
$D(B)$ exactly, or at a cap. Byzantine validators can report $D(B)$
without holding the block; the effect is an early proposal and a lost
fast path, never a safety issue. The comparison that matters is against
a fixed delay of the same mean, not against proposing immediately.

\textbf{Synchronization.} Inside consensus a validator knows the ids it
lacks and fetches them; no sketch is needed (Fig.~\ref{fig:sync}, left).
Sketch-sized IBLTs~\cite{goodrich2011iblt} matter where no id list exists:
mempool reconciliation between peers, partition healing, and fork
healing. IBLT short ids are salted per session, as in Erlay, so an
adversary cannot grind colliding ids in advance; the decoder knows only
its own items and fetches the rest in one further round.

\textbf{Partition detection and healing.} A partition makes the sketch
cloud bimodal. Camp centroids are exact by linearity, the inter-camp
$\hat d = \norm{\mu_{1} - \mu_{2}}^{2}/n$ sizes the repair before it
starts, and one estimate-then-decode exchange between representatives
gives both camps the union.

\textbf{Fork localization.} Prefix accumulators $P[b] = \sum_{b' \le b}
D(\mathrm{block}_{b'})$ make every range digest a subtraction. Two nodes
find their fork point by binary search on LtHash equality of prefixes in
$\lceil \log_{2} H \rceil + 1$ probes, and each probe's sketch reports how
much diverged. The structure is that of range-based set
reconciliation~\cite{meyer2023rbsr}; the size estimate per probe is the
addition.

\textbf{Gossip-averaged agreement.} Because sketches are linear, push-sum
gossip~\cite{kempe2003gossip} lets every validator estimate the mean
sketch $\mu$ and the dispersion $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\norm{D_{j} -
\mu}^{2}/n$ without an aggregator. The dispersion plays the role of
$1 - r^{2}$ for the Kuramoto order parameter $r$ of coupled oscillators,
which inspired this project's name; we claim the analogy as a metric
only, and a Byzantine-robust gossip variant is future work.

\section{Cross-Shard Conservation}
\label{sec:conservation}

Each cross-shard transaction $t$ from shard $i$ to shard $j$, debited at
block $h$, adds the ledger digest of the corridor-keyed item
$(i, j, t)$ to $i$'s outbound accumulator $O_{ij}[h]$; when $j$ applies
the credit, whose message carries $h$, the same item enters $j$'s inbound
accumulator $I_{ij}[h]$. Each block, every shard's quorum signs its
aggregate outbound and inbound digests over \emph{settled} cohorts
($h \le \mathrm{now} - W$), and the beacon checks
\begin{equation}
\textstyle\sum_{i} O_{i}^{\le \mathrm{now} - W} \;=\;
\sum_{j} I_{j}^{\le \mathrm{now} - W}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Threat model.} Each shard's quorum attests honestly to what the
shard applied; shard-internal execution is secured by its own BFT
instance. The faults detected are in the cross-shard path: dropped,
replayed, minted, and misrouted credits from faulty or Byzantine
relayers and bridges. A Byzantine shard quorum could sign accumulators
that misreport its own execution; catching that needs state-validity or
fraud proofs and is out of scope.

\begin{theorem}[Conservation]
\label{thm:conservation}
Under this threat model, if the settled invariant holds then the multiset
of settled debits equals the multiset of settled credits, per corridor,
unless an LtHash collision has been found.
\end{theorem}

\begin{IEEEproof}
Both sides are LtHash sums over corridor-keyed items. If the multisets
differ, the two sums are a collision for distinct multisets of
LtHash inputs.
\end{IEEEproof}

Three design choices carry the result, and each repairs a failure of our
earlier drafts. \emph{Corridor keying}: without it, a credit applied on
the wrong corridor adds the same vector the debit added and the global
invariant still balances; with it, a misroute is a drop on one corridor
and a mint on another. \emph{Cohort indexing}: credits trail debits by
network latency, so comparing whole accumulators raises alarms on every
busy corridor; comparing only settled cohorts raises none. \emph{Group
testing}: on violation the beacon bisects the corridor set with
aggregated settled digests; corridor keying makes cancellation inside a
group require a collision, so the recursion is exact and uses at most
$2f\lceil \log_{2}(C/f) \rceil + 1$ probes for $f$ faulty corridors
among $C$. A multiset-aware IBLT over the settled debits and credits of
an alarmed corridor then names its dangling transactions; a replayed
credit decodes as one extra.

The semantics are detection and settlement verification, not atomicity:
pair the invariant with optimistic execution and a revert window, or
credit only on match.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\textbf{Method.} The artifact accounts every message under one cost
model shared by all protocols: the proposal (64\,B plus 32\,B per
transaction id) reaches all $N$ validators, votes are 128\,B, a
certificate is a 96\,B aggregate plus 64\,B of metadata and an $N/8$-byte
signer bitmap (or no bitmap with threshold signatures), stragglers fetch
by id at 232\,B per transaction, Byzantine validators abstain, every
certificate reaches every validator, and we track bytes arriving at the
leader. Signatures are verified: a hash-based verifiable mock at scale,
real BLS12-381 in the tests. This is a counting simulator, not a network
emulator; it reports no latency. Unless stated, 30\% of validators are
Byzantine at random positions, $p_{\mathrm{miss}} = 0.37$, and blocks
carry 21 transactions. Baselines are HotStuff, HotStuff-2, chained
HotStuff amortized per block, a message model of Kauri (fanout 10,
without pipelining or reconfiguration), and PBFT.

\subsection{Conservation}

In a 16-shard ring run with 5120 cross-shard transactions, credits
landing one block after debits (640 still in flight at the check), and
six injected faults (two drops, a replay, a mint, and a misroute), the
settled invariant failed with a dangling estimate of 5.9 against 6; the
aged alarms named exactly the five affected corridors (the misroute
implicates two), bisection found them in 21 probes, and the decode
recovered every dangling transaction exactly. The same run without
faults raised no alarm. Fig.~\ref{fig:conservation} (right) shows

4\,KB ledger digests per shard per block, independent of volume and of
the corridor graph. Per-corridor batch roots cost 256\,B per corridor:
on a ring they are 32 times cheaper at every size; on a full mesh,
conservation is cheaper from $S = 34$ shards on. Batched receipts grow
with volume on top of the per-corridor roots.

\section{Corrections to the Earlier Version}
\label{sec:errata}

For readers of~\cite{mercier2026proxima} and of our earlier drafts:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The one-round fast path at $2N/3$ signatures is unsafe across view
changes (Section~\ref{sec:consensus}, Table~\ref{tab:fuzz}). The safe
quorum is $\lceil (n + 3f + 1)/2 \rceil$ with vote histories in
\textsc{new-view}.
\item The liveness bound $\exp(-2N(1/3 - p)^{2})$ ignores Byzantine
abstainers and is wrong near $f = N/3$ (Section~\ref{sec:consensus}).
\item Message counts $2\times$ below HotStuff did not deliver
certificates to all validators, charged different retransmission costs,
and omitted chained HotStuff; under a shared model there is no message
advantage over HotStuff-2 (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item The aggregator-chosen and, in our drafts, median references give
the aggregator discretion; inside consensus the reference is $D(B)$.
\item Tree leaves placed Byzantine validators last, so no leaf leader
was ever Byzantine; with random placement leaves need a fallback path.
\item Binding of the $\pm 1$ accumulator cannot rest on the modulus
(Proposition~\ref{prop:inert}); the ledger now binds with LtHash.
\item Deflation is not bounded by constant factors
(Proposition~\ref{prop:deflate}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{No network measurements.} All results are counts from a
simulator; latency, throughput, and the real distribution of
$p_{\mathrm{miss}}$ need a multi-region deployment.

\textbf{Proofs.} Theorem~\ref{thm:safety} is a proof sketch backed by
fuzzing of single-slot agreement; a mechanized proof (TLA+ or Ivy) and a
multi-slot, pipelined variant are open.

\textbf{Robust estimation.} Commit-then-salt restores unbiasedness
against chosen sets; integrating it into gossip without an extra round,
and Byzantine-robust push-sum over 512-dimensional sketches, are open.

\textbf{Applications.} The most promising use we see outside this paper
is censorship monitoring for Ethereum inclusion lists~\cite{eip7805}:
committee members publish sketches of the transactions they consider
eligible, the median estimates how many a block omitted, and full lists
are sent only when that estimate trips. Mempool divergence already causes
false censorship accusations~\cite{albrecht2026mempool}; sketches
measure it. Replica-consistency checks that report divergence size, and
relay completeness scoring for gossip networks, are similar.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A hash tells a consensus protocol whether views differ; a sketch tells it
roughly how much, and an adversary who knows the salt can make that
estimate say almost anything. Taking both facts seriously gives a narrow,
useful role for measurement in BFT: deciding when to try a fast path,
whom to synchronize, and where a partition or a broken shard corridor
is, with every decision failing soft and every safety property resting
on signatures and on an LtHash accumulator. Building that role carefully
exposed an unsafe fast path, a wrong liveness bound, an inert modulus,
and an evaluation that flattered our earlier work; the corrected system
costs more bytes than HotStuff-2 and is worth it only where its
observability is.
